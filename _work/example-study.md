## The Problem

Choosing a college major or career path can be difficult, especially when students aren't sure how their interests and skills translate into future opportunities.

BobaTalks, a nonprofit mentorship program focused on helping students navigate career and personal development, wanted to create a free resource to make that process a little easier.

Our team set out to build a short, interactive quiz that would help high school and early college students explore potential majors and careers based on their interests, hobbies, and skills.

But before getting too far into the design, we needed to better understand how students were currently making these decisions and whether a tool like this would actually be useful.

## My Role

As the sole UX Researcher, I worked with a cross-functional team of researchers, designers, product managers, and developers throughout the project.

My work included creating a screener survey, recruiting participants for user interviews, and working with the design team to understand how research findings could inform the product.

Throughout the project, we used different research methods to better understand students' experiences and identify opportunities to improve the Major Guide.

## Understanding the Problem

**Starting with our assumptions**

Before conducting research, our team outlined the assumptions we had about students and how they choose their majors and careers.

We wanted to understand how students explored their options, what resources they relied on, and whether their interests played a role in the decisions they made.

**Screener survey**

I created a screener survey to learn more about students' experiences choosing majors and careers, the resources they used, and their interest in a potential Major Guide.

One interesting takeaway was that many respondents reported already knowing what major they wanted to pursue. This raised questions about how useful our proposed tool would be for students who had already chosen a path.

We also found that 47% of respondents reported using third-party websites to explore majors and careers.

While the survey helped us understand how students approached these decisions, we wanted to dig deeper into their experiences.

**User interviews**

Following the survey, I reached out to seven participants for interviews.

These conversations revealed something interesting: even when students had a major in mind, their path wasn't always straightforward.

Among the seven interview participants:

All seven said they wished they had a tool to help them identify their interests before switching majors.

All seven described taking a course to explore an interest, only to discover whether they liked or disliked the subject.

The interviews gave us a different perspective from the initial survey results. Rather than focusing only on helping students choose a major, we began to see an opportunity to help them explore their interests before committing to a particular path.



## Informing the Design Direction

**Learning from existing tools**

With a better understanding of students' experiences, we worked with the design team to conduct a competitive analysis of existing major and career exploration tools.

We looked at how other products helped students discover their interests, what information they provided, and how their experiences were structured.

One pattern we noticed was that many of the quizzes we reviewed were lengthy and required students to answer quite a few questions before receiving results.

This gave our team a direction to explore: Could we create a shorter experience that still helped students find relevant majors and careers?

The competitive analysis helped inform the team's initial designs and gave us a starting point for usability testing.

## Evaluating the Experience

Once the initial designs were ready, we conducted two rounds of usability testing to understand how students interacted with the Major Guide.

Moderated usability testing

We conducted moderated usability sessions with five participants over Zoom. These sessions gave us an opportunity to observe how people navigated the experience, understand where they struggled, and ask follow-up questions.

Unmoderated usability testing

We also conducted unmoderated usability testing with 23 participants through Maze. This gave us additional feedback on the experience and helped us identify potential usability issues.

Following testing, we used affinity mapping and reviewed the Maze results to identify patterns in the feedback.

We then worked with the design team to determine which areas of the experience needed to change.

## Key Findings and Design Changes

Finding 1: Students had difficulty comparing their options

What we found

During usability testing, we noticed that the cards displaying different options were taking up too much space on the page.

Participants had to scroll back and forth to review their choices, making it difficult to remember what they had already selected.

What changed

We reduced the size of the cards so that students could view more options at once while keeping the content readable.

This was intended to make it easier for students to compare their choices without constantly scrolling.

Finding 2: Students wanted more options to explore

What we found

Some participants couldn't find classes or subjects they were interested in. Others noticed that options they were already familiar with weren't represented.

This suggested that the available selections didn't fully reflect the range of interests students wanted to explore.

What changed

We expanded the available class options using university course listings and other information gathered during the research.

The goal was to give students more opportunities to find subjects that matched their interests.

Finding 3: Students wanted to explore majors without completing the quiz

What we found

Students didn't have a straightforward way to browse major and career information from the main page.

To access that information, they had to go through the quiz experience.

What changed

We added an "All Majors and Careers" section below the quiz, giving students another way to explore the available information.

This meant students could browse majors and careers directly instead of having to complete the quiz first.

This was an important change because it gave students more flexibility in how they explored their options.

## Research Impact
Throughout the project, our research helped the team identify usability issues, question some of our initial assumptions, and make changes to the Major Guide.

The findings influenced several design decisions:

Making selection cards smaller to reduce excessive scrolling.

Expanding available class options to better reflect students' interests.

Adding a way to explore majors and careers without completing the quiz.

One of the more important outcomes of the research was recognizing that students might want to explore their options in different ways.

Rather than relying entirely on the quiz, the team introduced another path for students who wanted to browse majors and careers on their own.

While we identified and addressed several usability issues, further testing would be needed to understand how effective the updated experience was.


## Reflections
One of my biggest takeaways from this project was learning how to balance the depth of research with the time available.

We used several different methods throughout the project, and each helped us understand the problem from a different perspective. At the same time, coordinating the research and working through the findings took time, especially with multiple teams involved.

Looking back, I would have spent more time identifying our biggest research questions early on and prioritizing which methods would help us answer them.

This project also reminded me why it's important to challenge our assumptions. What we initially thought students needed wasn't necessarily the full picture, and talking directly with them helped us better understand the different ways they approached choosing a major or career.
